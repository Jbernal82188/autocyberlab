With wireless relay theft targeting push-to-start vehicles across North America and Europe, **Faraday pouches and RF-shielding boxes** have become essential security gear for car owners. However, independent RF testing shows that nearly 40% of budget pouches sold online fail to block key fob signals entirely—or lose their shielding effectiveness after just a few weeks in your pocket.

To choose a Faraday enclosure that actually stops relay amplifiers, you need to understand the exact radio frequencies your smart key uses and why standard shielding fails.

## 1. The Exact Frequencies a Car Key Faraday Pouch Must Block

A common mistake is testing a Faraday bag by placing a smartphone inside and calling it. **Blocking a cellular signal does not mean your car key is safe.** Smartphones operate on higher microwave frequencies (850 MHz to 5 GHz), whereas automotive Passive Keyless Entry (PKE) systems rely on a dual-band architecture that is much harder to attenuate:

1. **Low Frequency Interrogation (LF - 125 kHz / 134.2 kHz):** Your car's door handle antennas transmit a magnetic induction pulse at 125 kHz to wake up the key fob. Low-frequency magnetic fields easily penetrate thin, single-layer metallic fabrics.
2. **Ultra High Frequency Response (UHF - 315 MHz in US/Canada, 433.92 MHz / 868 MHz in Europe):** Once awakened by the 125 kHz pulse, the fob transmits its encrypted rolling code over UHF.
3. **Ultra-Wideband (UWB - 6.5 GHz to 8 GHz):** Used in newer luxury vehicles for Time-of-Flight distance bounding.

If a cheap pouch reduces the 315/433 MHz UHF signal by only 20 dB and fails to block the **125 kHz wake-up pulse**, a thief using a high-gain directional antenna near your front door can still wake the key fob and capture its reply.

## 2. What Makes a True Automotive-Grade Faraday Bag?

 Effective RF shielding across both LF and UHF bands requires specific materials and seam geometry:

* **Multi-Layer Copper-Nickel (Cu/Ni) Ripstop Mesh:** High-grade pouches use at least two to three layers of woven nickel and copper fabric, delivering **60 dB to 85+ dB of signal attenuation** across the 100 kHz to 10 GHz spectrum.
* **Roll-Top Overlap Closure:** Standard zippers cannot block radio waves. If the top flap does not fold over tightly to create a continuous metal-on-metal seal, the opening acts as a *slot antenna* and leaks RF energy.

## 3. Why Faraday Pouches Degrade Over Time (And the 10-Second Door Test)

Every time you sit on a Faraday pouch in your pocket or scrape metal house keys against the inner lining, the microscopic copper-nickel fibers fracture. Within months of daily wear, micro-gaps form in the mesh.

### The Mandatory Monthly Verification Test
1. Place your key fob inside the inner shielded pocket of the pouch (never the outer unshielded slip pocket) and seal the flap tightly.
2. Walk directly up to your locked vehicle and hold the pouch within **4 inches (10 cm) of the driver's door handle**.
3. Pull the handle or press the keyless request button.
4. **The Verdict:** If the doors unlock or the mirrors unfold, **your pouch has failed** and is no longer blocking 125 kHz proximity polling.

## Conclusion

For carrying your key in your pocket away from home, invest in a dual-layer Cu/Ni pouch with a fold-over velcro seal and avoid storing sharp metal keys in the same compartment. For overnight storage at home, a **rigid Faraday box** lined with thick RF foam and metallic shielding is far superior because its walls never flex or wear out.
